Alvin Morgan

1894 – 1965

Birth: 17 Apr 1894 Sanford, Colorado
Marriage: 23 Oct 1924 Alamosa, Colorado
Death: 06 Oct 1965 La Jara, Colorado

Biographical Sketch

I was only 22 in 1965 when my father died as a result of a blood clot, so my life experience with him was very short. He was born April 17, 1894 in Sanford, Colorado and the fourth son of Nielsine Katherine Jensen (1869 - 1928) and John William Morgan (1862 - 1928). He attended grade school in Sanford and was a good student. Unfortunately, due to family poverty he did not advance much more above the 8th grade.

Unfortunately I do not know much about his growing up years but they were full of hard work and family trials. He almost died of diphtheria when his younger sister Geneva died at age 9. That was a very sad time.

He was gifted in knowing how to make do out of whatever he could find, and his creativity helped make life easier for my mother, and most likely for his mother before that also as he did help take care of his grandmother. (She was a native of Denmark so he learned some Danish, however all I remember was he could count in Danish.) I do know he really loved doing things to make my mother happy, and he whistled when he painted. Together they created a family centered home, and a home that welcomed anyone who knocked at the door.

They had the house one block south of the Sanford School when I was born. It was old but clean and my dad always tried to make it comfortable with his gifted painting and wall paper ability. He enjoyed being home and enjoyed my mother's southern cooking and so did his two brothers, Ed and Lew as well as anyone else who showed up including cousins. Good food was readily available. My dad loved sharing, therefore, my mother always cooked large portions.

The house my dad grew up in was small, and with so many boys, sharing was a natural occurrence. In fact he and his brother, Lew, shared a car they purchased with their military sign up bonus. The oldest boy, Melvin, was already married at that time.

Before my dad was married at age 30 he already had all the adventures of life away from Sanford that he cared to have. My Uncle Ed wrote a history of his adventures which did include some encounters with my dad in California.

I do know at one time my dad had an Indian motorcycle. He was in an accident and it was severe enough that he had a near death experience in which a voice told him it was not his time to die. He never rode that motorcycle again. It was no longer a thrill for him.

My Uncle Lew and my dad signed up to join the army and where Lew went I don't know. But my dad ended up in a hospital in France with the Spanish Flu or pneumonia. He was not engaged in too much fighting but saw the horrific results. He did not talk about it but I do know personally he hated war movies. He preferred the singing and dancing ones, and so did I.

My Uncle Ed and Aunt Eva were married a few months before my parents marriage. They were the witnesses at their wedding in Alamosa. Apparently wedding celebrations were not big

deals to them. However, in November 1960 when my parents were finally able to go to the temple in Salt Lake City my dad was thrilled. I was sitting by them on the trip back to Colorado while my dad was kissing my mother like a new bride. I was 17. I just thought he was silly but now I understand their happiness of having an eternal marriage. My dad only lived 5 more years after that. He died in LaJara, Colorado on October 6, 1965.

The house was on a large enough small town lot that there was room for animals and a large garden. My parents worked hard to provide food for the table and plenty for storage. It was strictly organic. Manure was the fertilizer for the garden and the flowers. My dad cut down an old car called the Bug, as he had no money for a tractor. It had no decent brakes but ran very slow for making rows in the garden and pulling a hay cutter, rake or wagon. I learned to drive straight between rows of hay on the Bug. The driving was fun but pitching hay was hard work and very itchey.

My dad milked a cow until he died. Once again they shared the milk and meat with my brother and oldest sister who lived in Sanford also. He also knew how to cut up meat because after he and his brother butchered the steer or the pig he would see that pieces were packaged before it went into the local freezer locker in LaJara, Colorado. When the slaughter house opened in LaJara the meat came home all neatly packaged and ready for our home freezer. I was happy about that as I would have nightmares about seeing a pig head in a pan in the bathtub awaiting my mother making head cheese. That was a type of lunch meat she would make which was a recipe with two ingredients I did not like -- sage and raw onions. I have no idea what else went into it but a pig head did make a lot of head cheese. Apparently my mother made delicious sausage too. Once again it had sage so was no favorite of mine.

During my childhood my dad worked for the Denver and Rio Grande Railroad as a painter. That may be where he developed his perfect skills of painting so well he left no brush strokes. He worked steadily until I was in the sixth grade and then he experienced several months of being laid off. So from November until the end of February we went to Mississippi to stay with my sister, Genevieve as she recovered from surgery. As I look back that experience was a big sacrifice for my parents. Genevieve's little house was a lot like a three room shack. It had no bathroom or hot water. It did have a metal sink with drawers and the stove was gas. Water was heated in a pan on the stove. We went to a laundromat on Saturdays to wash our clothes.

I attended sixth grade at Stringer Elementary. A bus picked me up right in front of the house so transportation was no problem. I was welcomed into the class by a Mrs Stringer and many courteous classmates.

My dad did try to make a few improvements by building a better "outhouse" and a few cupboards in the kitchen. Ironically Genevieve and Malcolm were among the first in their community to have a television. We had no television at home so my parents must have enjoyed passing time enjoying that novelty and tending their precious granddaughter.

We stayed in Mississippi until the end of February. My Uncle Melvin Morgan became ill and my brother called to tell my parents they needed to come home because my uncle was not expected to live. We left immediately for Colorado.

As it turned out my Uncle outlived my dad, so he wasn't as ill as previously thought. My parents were glad to be home though and so was I.

When I was in high school my dad retired from the railroad and enjoyed staying home and tending to the demands of the mini farm. My parents purchased a TV in 1958. My dad had a favorite rocking chair and relaxed by napping while my mother watched some of our favorite shows. Their only disagreement was that my dad literally liked to go to bed early when the sun went down. My mother was more of a night owl.

They stayed busy with grandchildren who lived nearby and also worked on improving their surroundings. The big garden was a showpiece for the neighborhood and my parents were always sharing their bounty. They were always kind and generous and loved each other very much.

Because nieces and nephews were around a lot my dad put a lock on my upstairs door so I could enjoy some privacy. He also let me have a typewriter for my 17 th birthday. That was a very big deal in that day and age. It helped me so much in college and even after I got married. He loved making others happy-especially my mother.

In 1960 after my dad received his Veterans and Railroad pensions he bought a new red Ford Falcon. That was the only new car he ever owned. He was so proud of it and was like a little boy with a new red wagon. He died in 1965 and the red Falcon now is owned by a grandson who was only a baby in 1960.

Written by his daughter Barbara
